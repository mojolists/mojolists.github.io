## At Release

This was certainly not an easy listen for the average record buyer in 1971. Arriving as a sprawling double LP filled with avant-garde jams and tape manipulation, it left traditional rock critics mostly baffled. Tracks routinely stretched past the fifteen-minute mark, abandoning standard verse-chorus structures entirely in favor of relentless, driving repetition and spontaneous vocal improvisations.

While it did manage to find a small, dedicated underground audience in Europe, the wider world wasn't quite sure what to make of it. The combination of Holger Czukay's meticulous audio splicing and the band's chaotic, live energy in a German castle felt far too alien and unstructured to crack mainstream radio or commercial charts.

## The Influence

The Cologne quartet essentially invented post-rock, motorik groove, and experimental studio technique simultaneously and nobody in the mainstream noticed for twenty years. Radiohead absorbed it. LCD Soundsystem absorbed it. Mark E. Smith of The Fall talked about it constantly. The drum work alone — Jaki Liebezeit's locked, hypnotic groove — changed how people thought about rhythm in rock music.

Vocalist Damo Suzuki also introduced a completely new way of interacting with a band. Rather than singing clear lyrics over the music, he used his voice as an additional rhythm instrument, mumbling, whispering, and shouting in ways that directly informed the approach of future ambient and experimental frontmen.

## Where It Stands Today

The record is now viewed as the holy grail of the krautrock movement. The steady, pulsating rhythms that seemed so bizarre in the early seventies are now fundamental building blocks for modern indie, electronic, and post-punk acts.

Far from being forgotten, its reputation has skyrocketed in the internet era. As contemporary music production has become increasingly focused on groove, texture, and loop-based structures, modern audiences have realized that this group was essentially mapping out the future of audio half a century in advance.
